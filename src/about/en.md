I spent my early years in San Lorenzo, Puerto Rico, collecting atlases and reading every encyclopedia I could find, already convinced that the world was full of questions worth chasing.

For most of my childhood, I wanted to be a veterinarian. Then, in high school, I stumbled into research. Two science fair projects later, I had presented my work in Los Angeles and Hangzhou, and something had permanently shifted. I wasn't just curious about the world anymore. I wanted to be one of the people figuring it out.

That journey took me from tackling Puerto Rico's drinking water crisis, to studying meteoritic chemistry at NASA Goddard, to developing a molecular biosignature framework for future life-detection missions during my PhD at Georgia Tech. The questions kept getting bigger. I kept following them.

Along the way, I learned something no lab could teach me. Through community work in some of Puerto Rico's most underserved neighborhoods, bringing science into afterschool programs, community centers, and public festivals, I saw firsthand how much a little exposure can change a person's sense of what's possible. That shaped a conviction I carry into everything I do: advancing science and opening access to it are not separate goals. They are the same goal.

Today I'm a Research Scientist II in the Planetary Exploration Laboratory at Georgia Tech, working where astrobiology, quantum chemistry, and instrument development meet. I communicate that work in classrooms, at conferences, on social media, and at public events, in English and Spanish. From 2025 to 2026, I also led ExplOrigins, Georgia Tech's astrobiology and space exploration student organization, as its president.

I'm always looking for collaborators, partners, and conversations at that same meeting point of rigorous science and broad human impact.

When I'm not doing science or talking about it, you'll find me behind a camera, lost in a film, or planning a trip somewhere I've never been.
