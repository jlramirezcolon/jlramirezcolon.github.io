## Background

Polyphenols are natural compounds made by plants. They are valued as antioxidants and used in food, medicine, cosmetics, adhesives, plastics, and more. Runoff from cities, farms, and industry carries them into rivers and wetlands, where they can transform into highly toxic pollutants that harm ecosystems and people.

## The problem

Membrane filtration is a standard tool in water treatment, and the goal here was a membrane that treatment facilities could use to capture polyphenols before treated water is released into the ocean. The membranes most often used to remove pollutants are made of synthetic polymers such as polysulfone and polyamides. They work, but because they repel water, they tend to clog and let water through slowly.

Cellulose acetate, made from natural cellulose, is nontoxic and inexpensive, and it is hydrophilic: it attracts water, so water flows through it more easily. Its drawback is that the fibers it forms have uneven pores that do not hold on to target molecules well.

## What we did

We used electrospinning, a technique that draws a polymer solution into very fine fibers with an electric field, to make highly porous cellulose acetate membranes. We then coated them with a block copolymer, PEO-b-P4VP, that can grab polyphenols through hydrogen bonds, and characterized the result with electron microscopy, spectroscopy, and tensile testing.

To test the membranes, we used two model polyphenols, catechol and gallic acid, chosen because they break down slowly and are highly toxic in water. We ran adsorption experiments and solid-phase extraction using real water from the Ciénaga Las Cucharillas, a wetland in Puerto Rico.

## What we found

The membranes recovered both polyphenols at concentrations in the parts-per-million range. They captured more gallic acid than catechol, consistent with gallic acid having more places to form hydrogen bonds. Used for solid-phase extraction, the membranes also recovered catechol from the wetland water. Together, the results point to a way of recovering these compounds before they turn into toxic pollutants, and possibly reusing them.

## My role

I carried out this work over four years as an undergraduate researcher in the WARM2 Laboratory at the University of Puerto Rico, Río Piedras, and I am first author of the resulting article in *ACS Omega* (2022). I also presented it at national and Puerto Rican scientific meetings, and the work earned an AAAS Emerging Researchers National (ERN) Conference in STEM Oral Session Award in the Chemical Sciences category.
