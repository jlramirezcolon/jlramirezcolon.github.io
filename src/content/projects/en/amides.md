## Background

Carbonaceous chondrites are meteorites that come from asteroids or comets and have changed very little since the Solar System formed. They carry organic compounds such as amino acids, carboxylic acids, and nucleobases, and they may have delivered these ingredients to the early Earth.

Aliphatic amides are a family of simple molecules made of four of the elements life needs most: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen. The simplest, formamide, has been found in comets, in the space between stars, and toward the center of our galaxy. In the lab, formamide mixed with minerals, clays, or meteorite powder can form nucleobases, amino acids, carboxylic acids, and amines.

## The problem

Surprisingly, no amide had ever been reported in a meteorite. Finding them would matter: amides have been proposed as precursors of other organic molecules found in meteorites, and their presence would support the idea that meteorites carried the starting materials for prebiotic chemistry.

Amides are also hard to measure. They are highly polar and do not evaporate easily, so before they can be analyzed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS), they have to be chemically modified, a step called derivatization.

## What we did

As a Research Associate in Astrobiology at NASA Goddard's Astrobiology Analytical Laboratory in the summer of 2019, I worked closely with José Aponte, under the supervision of Jamie Elsila and Jason Dworkin, to develop a derivatization method for eleven aliphatic amides and urea. We ran more than a thousand reactions, testing four derivatization reagents under different temperatures, reaction times, solvents, and catalysts, and found one reagent, MSTFA, that gave the highest yields with the fewest byproducts.

We then tested the method on heated Allende meteorite powder spiked with amide standards, to see whether meteorite minerals would interfere, and finally on a 200 mg sample of the Murchison meteorite.

## What we found

The minerals caused no decomposition of the amides and no significant byproducts. In Murchison, we saw a tentative signal of acetamide, but it may have been a false positive: the solvent we used, acetonitrile, can react with traces of water to form acetamide during extraction or derivatization. No amide could be clearly identified, which pointed to the next step: controlling water during the process, for example with molecular sieves or drying agents.

I presented this work at the American Geophysical Union Fall Meeting (*Silylation Protocol for the Analysis of Meteoritic Amides*) and at the 2020 Europlanet Science Congress (*Towards an Effective Method for the Analysis of Meteoritic Amides*).
