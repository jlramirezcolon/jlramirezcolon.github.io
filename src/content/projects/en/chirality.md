## Background

Many molecules come in two mirror-image forms, like a left and a right hand: the same atoms, connected the same way, but impossible to stack perfectly on top of each other. For amino acids, the two forms are called L- and D-. Life on Earth uses mainly L-amino acids, while chemistry without life tends to make both forms in equal amounts.

That is why an excess of one form over the other is one of the best-known amino acid biosignatures, and why it is routinely measured in meteorites and in samples returned from asteroids.

## The problem

Because mirror-image molecules have identical chemical properties, telling them apart is hard. Standard methods rely on polarized light or on other chiral molecules that act as "recognizers," and often need careful sample preparation. For space missions and precious returned samples, it would help to have a method that is compact, sensitive, and purely electrical.

## What we did

Led by our collaborators at Osaka University, the team used the same kind of nanogap sensor behind ELIE. With atomically sharp gold tips and machine learning, the blip a molecule leaves in the tunneling current can reveal not only which amino acid it is, but also which hand. The team tested this on the L- and D- forms of many amino acids, and then on real extracts from the Murchison meteorite and from the Atacama Desert in Chile.

## What we found

The nanogap distinguished individual L- and D- amino acids with over 80% accuracy. In a blind test of 39 amino acids, it identified both the molecule and its hand with more than 50% accuracy, well above chance, and it quantified mixtures of four amino acids with more than 66% accuracy. In the meteorite and desert extracts, using a panel of 11 amino acids, the nanogap results captured the major features seen with liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS), a gold standard for this type of analysis.

## My role

As a Visiting Researcher in NASA Goddard's Astrobiology Analytical Laboratory in April 2024, I worked with Hannah McLain to extract amino acids from the Murchison meteorite and the Atacama Desert samples, using cleanroom protocols, and to analyze them with LC-MS. Those measurements were the reference the nanogap results were compared against. The work was published in *Nature Communications* in 2026.
