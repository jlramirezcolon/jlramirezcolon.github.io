## Background

The mangrove oyster (*Crassostrea rhizophorae*), a species native to Caribbean and South American Atlantic coasts, lives attached to the roots of red mangroves in estuaries, where rivers meet the sea. It is a local delicacy, but also a remarkably efficient natural filter: as it feeds, it strains phytoplankton, bacteria, algae, pollutants, and toxins out of the water.

A pilot project in San Juan's Condado Lagoon had already shown that transplanting these oysters lowered the water's turbidity.

## The problem

Puerto Rico is a small, densely populated island, and as cities grow, more contaminants reach its rivers, lagoons, and beaches. The island needs safe, low-cost ways to restore its waters, ideally ones that work with the ecosystem rather than against it.

My question was how well native mangrove oysters could filter the water of La Torrecilla Lagoon in Carolina, which is less salty than where they usually live, and whether murkier water would slow them down.

## What I did

I collected water from La Torrecilla Lagoon and filled four aquariums with about 15 liters each, at four turbidity levels: a control matching the lagoon itself, and three increasingly murky tanks. I added about 25–30 oysters to each tank and measured turbidity, pH, and dissolved oxygen every hour.

In a second round, I added more sediment-laden lagoon water to three of the tanks, pushing them above 100 NTU, and measured them every half hour. Afterward, the oysters were released into a suitable habitat.

## What I found

In under two hours, the oysters filtered all 15 liters in every tank. In the control, turbidity dropped from 10.9 to 0.7 NTU, and in the murkiest tank of the first round, from 44 to 1.9 NTU. In the second round, the murkiest tank went from 216.4 to 33.2 NTU in a single hour.

Dissolved oxygen stayed between about 6.3 and 6.5 ppm throughout, comfortably healthy for the oysters. The lagoon water itself started at 3.57 ppm, a sign of the poor conditions life there faces.

My hypothesis was only partly right. I expected the murkiest tanks to clear the least, but some cleared faster than expected, likely because they held more mature oysters, which filter more water. The experiment also showed that mangrove oysters filter well in water less salty than their usual habitat, pointing to a natural, low-cost way to help clean the island's waters.

## Recognition

The project was recognized as one of the top five in North America for the Community Impact Award at the Google Science Fair 2016. It also won first place at the Metropolitan Science Fair and the U.S. Stockholm Junior Water Prize Regional Award, among other awards.
